# Guided fixes you talk through (no remote access)

Chapter 2 was about not grabbing the device when you're sitting next to them. This chapter is the phone-call version, and it has one non-negotiable rule:

**You never remotely access their device. And you spend part of one early visit teaching them to never let anyone else do it, either.**

That wall protects three things at once:

- **Their learning.** A fix they watched happen on "your control of my screen" is a fix they can't repeat. The whole short-term gain evaporates by next week.
- **Their trust and their dignity.** Letting someone steer your own device feels safe with a relative — and most elder scams *start* by building that exact permission. You matter more as the person who taught the wall than as someone who occasionally stands behind it.
- **You.** Being the person with access becomes a job. "Can you just pop in and…" is a life sentence, usually at dinnertime.

So the toolset is words. The good news: words work better than people expect.

## The talk-through method

The core trick is called **be their screen**. They describe; you see. Not the reverse.

1. **Start every talk-through the same way.** *"Tell me what you see, top to bottom, like a picture — colors, words, buttons."* Don't lead with what you think is there. You'll both start believing in an imaginary screen.
2. ** Assume nothing about which device.** "The internet" means the router, the TV, the laptop, or the news app — clarify with *"Which screen are you looking at right now?"*
3. **Locate, then act.** First get them to say where they are (*"I found the gear"*), *then* give one action: *"Tap the word 'Camera,' then stop."* One instruction, then silence. People need silence to read.
4. **Use landmarks, not directions.** *"The button that looks like a paper plane"* survives badly-worded menus; *"top right"* doesn't. Landmarks are also how they'll find it again tomorrow on their own.
5. **Ban the word "no"** on your side. Wrong turns get: *"Not that one — and notice what happened, that tells us something."* The turn-safety habit from [chapter 2](02-teach-without-taking-over.md) pays for itself here.
6. **Read error text exactly.** Not "it made a sound" — the words. Exact words are how prompts like "Subscription required" turn out to be the real problem after ten wrong theories.

## The five-question open (phone-cut version)

The [full triage](01-support-call-checklist.md) needs paper. On a walkway call, here's the compressed version:

1. "What are you trying to do?"
2. "Did it work yesterday?"
3. "What'd you change?"
4. "What do the words on the screen say?"
5. "Anything with money or codes going on right now?" — that one catches RED calls in five seconds. If yes, drop everything and go to the [scam chapter](06-elder-scam-shields.md).

## The talk-through fix library

Three situations cover most calls. The pattern for each: **say, wait, hear, respond.**

### 1. "Nothing is happening" (frozen-looking)

- *"Swipe all the way up from the bottom — see every open app — swipe each card up and away."* Then have them reopen the one app fresh.
- Out loud, name the move so it becomes memorized: *"That's called 'force-closing an app.' Two-second fix, nobody knows it."*
- If the whole device is stuck: power off fully, power on, one sip of coffee, try again. If it's stuck three times in one week, that's a Sunday conversation, not five more resets.

### 2. "I clicked and now it's gone" (lost screen/lost thing)

- *"Press back once — one tap — now what do you see?"* Over-pressing back is the classic; teach **one back at a time**.
- Chrome/Safari tab bar if a page vanished; Recents on phone if a document vanished. Recent files section in Word, Gmail's "Sent," photos' "Recently deleted" (they're kept 30 days — say that sentence to a panicking person, it's a magic trick).
- Rule of thumb you're teaching: **nothing is gone-gone. Before panic: back, recents, and undo (Ctrl+Z).**

### 3. "It's asking for a password I don't know"

- First: is it the device, the app, or the account? (Device lock = passcode; app = account usually; different problems, different fixes.)
- Then: *"Let's use 'forgot password' right now instead of guessing. Guessing is how an account locks itself."*
- Whole passwords reset belongs with [chapter 5](05-passwords-and-backups.md) — half-fixed password problems on a call just hide the deeper mess. Say: *"This fix is ten minutes on Sunday and it never bothers us again. Tonight: don't lose mail, let's use your tablet meanwhile."*

## The two-retry rule and when to escalate

**Two clean attempts per fix.** Then you say out loud, without apology: *"We've hit the good limit over the phone. This needs a visit or the [real phone number for your ISP](07-when-to-call-the-isp-or-a-pro.md)."* That limit isn't defeat; it's what keeps the Wednesday-evening call from turning into Wednesday-evening surgery.

And remember the invisible option: **the visit takedown**. Some fixes only exist in person — set up the backup, rescue the printer, tour a scam text. If the call is on minute 25, saying *"this one is a coffee visit, Sunday at 2"* is the best move in your toolset, not a defeat.

## The line they can quote to scammers

While you're building the no-remote-access habit, give them the line to say to everyone else (this doubles as the script on the [scam teach sheet](teach-sheets/scam-scripts.md)):

> "I don't give access to my computer or my accounts on a phone call. I'll call my bank back on my own."

Calm, short, no debate needed. The people who never get scammed aren't the tech-savvy. They're the ones with one rehearsed sentence.
