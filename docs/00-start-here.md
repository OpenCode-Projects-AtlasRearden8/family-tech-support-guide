# Start here: the promise

You are the person your family calls. Not because you know everything about computers — because you are patient, and you never make them feel small about what they don't know.

This manual is written for that job. It is a field guide to being the calm, un-sighing family tech helper: the person who takes the call at 7 p.m. and turns "the printer is haunted" into "the printer is quietly working."

## The sigh is the thing that hurts

Here is the honest part. What people remember from a tech call is not the fix. It is your breathing. A tiny sigh when they say "wait, which button?" tells them *you think they're slow*. So they stop asking. That is how a small problem with the Wi-Fi becomes a year of quietly not using the laptop.

So the first rule, before any troubleshooting rule:

**Never sigh. Not out loud, not with your tone, not with your typing speed.**

If you feel the sigh coming, that's your cue to slow down and switch to one of the teaching moves in [chapter 2](02-teach-without-taking-over.md).

## The six rules of the calm helper

1. **Triage first, fix second.** Like a nurse on intake: is anything urgent, is any money moving, is anything blocking food/heat/medicine? Everything else waits its turn.
2. **Their hands, your words.** Never grab the phone or the mouse. If your fingers were supposed to be the fix, the fix leaves with you.
3. **No remote access, ever — and you don't use it either.** You talk them through fixes. You don't log into their devices, and you teach them *never* to let a stranger do it. (See [chapter 3](03-guided-fixes-you-talk-through.md).)
4. **One problem per call.** "The printer, my phone, and the internet are all broken" gets the rescue order, not a three-hour marathon. ([chapter 4](04-rescue-order.md))
5. **Passwords and backups happen on a visit, together.** These are the two things that prevent every future disaster. You book one hour and do both, side by side. ([chapter 5](05-passwords-and-backups.md))
6. **Know when it's not your job.** Some things belong to the internet company, a repair shop, or a lawyer. Sending it there calmly is a skill too. ([chapter 7](07-when-to-call-the-isp-or-a-pro.md))

## Book your office hours (protect the friendship, too)

Consistency beats heroics. Pick a rhythm you can actually keep — for example, "calls in the evening, one visit a month" — and offer it that way: *"I'm not always free, but I'm always free Sundays. Text me the problem and we'll do it Sunday."*

That framing does three things: it makes you reliable instead of on-demand, it stops 11 p.m. guilt spirals, and — not for nothing — it puts a small gap between the panic and the help. Half of all tech panics have solved themselves by the appointment time. Nobody is mad about the saved trip. (You will also see this idea in chapter 7: the passing of time is a legitimate repair tool.)

## The standard of done

A fix isn't done when it works once. It's done when:

- the person did the fix themselves (with you talking),
- they could find it again tomorrow, and
- a teach sheet (or a sticky note in their handwriting) is sitting near the thing.

If that sounds picky, remember the goal: one day you get a call that starts "I fixed it myself!" — and that call is the whole point of this manual.
