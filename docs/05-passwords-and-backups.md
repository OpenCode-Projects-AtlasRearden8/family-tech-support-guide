# Passwords and backups: the one big visit

These two are not the same kind of problem as a printer. Passwords and backups are the two things that decide how bad your family's *next* bad day is: whether a lost phone means a sad evening or the loss of every photo, every contact, and every place that saved a password in 2023.

They are also chores nobody wants to learn alone. So they happen **together, side by side, on a visit** — you book one unhurried hour, and you leave with both boxes ticked. Here's the hour.

## The visit agenda (one unhurried hour)

1. **0:00–0:10 — Settle in.** Both phones charging, coffee, the password notebook on the table if they keep one. Say the shape out loud: *"Today we spend an hour so that next time something breaks, nothing is lost."*
2. **0:10–0:30 — Passwords: pick the manager, change the two that matter.**
3. **0:30–0:40 — Backups: turn them on, on the spot.**
4. **0:40–0:50 — Test it.** A backup you've never restored is a rumor. Restore one photo back onto the phone together. One is enough to prove the chain works.
5. **0:50–0:60 — Leave the paper.** The [passwords teach sheet](teach-sheets/passwords.md) and [backups teach sheet](teach-sheets/backups.md) on the counter, next-visit skills picked, calendar updated.

## Passwords, in the only version worth doing

**Use the password manager that's already on their phone.** For most people that's the phone's built-in one (iCloud Keychain on iPhones, Google Password Manager on Android) — it fills passwords for them, syncs across their own devices, and asks for nothing extra. A separate app like Bitwarden or 1Password is better for some families, but "better" that never gets set up loses to "good and already running." Pick what they will actually have in six months.

- **The one password they must memorize** is the manager's (a phone passcode, a computer login, an Apple or Google account password). Make it **four unconnected words**: *maple-piano-basket-fly*. Four words beat a password with !@# in it, and it can be spoken to themselves while tapping it in.
- **Change the two that matter tonight:** the email account (it's the master key — who controls the email controls every "forgot password" button) and the bank. Everything else can happen naturally: from now on, every time they log into a site, the manager is there with the offer to *save* the password. That's the whole trick: you don't migrate. You let the phone collect, going forward.
- **Write the four words down.** Yes, on paper, in the notebook, next to nothing else. The right way to store one master password is paper in a drawer, not in a text file called "everything." (Say that out loud; it's counterintuitive and a relief.)
- **Turn on two-factor where it matters** (email, bank). Text-message codes are fine for beginners; the point is that it's on. Never walk them through buying a hardware key on visit one — that's a later-skill visit.

## Family safety rules (say these out loud on the visit)

- **You never type their passwords.** They tap; you look away or guide from memory of the *shape*, not the letters. People will hand you the phone mid-password — decline warmly every single time, so it becomes a house rule and not a judgment.
- **If they start telling you a password** out loud, stop them kindly: *"That one lives in your head or on that paper only — I'm not allowed to know it, and now you know I never will."*
- **Scammers will ask for codes.** Any code read over the phone to anyone is the whole theft. A fired-up rule for the household: **a code going out of the house is a fire alarm.** See [chapter 6](06-elder-scam-shields.md).

## Backups, done on the spot

The rule: **every photo and every contact should exist in two places that aren't the phone.** That's the whole backup philosophy for a household; anything fancier comes later or never.

- **iPhone:** Settings → their name → iCloud → the backup slider on; iCloud Photos on if there's room (buy the small plan if not — this is the one subscription worth its price; say that). A retired laptop running a full backup once a season is a nice bonus, not the requirement.
- **Android:** Settings → Google → Backup on; Google Photos backup on; check the two actually say "backed up today."
- **Computer:** a plug-in external drive running the built-in backup (Time Machine on a Mac, File History on Windows) — the one *local* copy that doesn't need an internet day to save you. Plug it in, turn it on, label the drive "BACKUP — keep plugged in."
- **Then test:** restore a photo back from the cloud onto the phone together, watch it appear, and name the skill: *"That's 'a backup we can trust.'"*
- **And the emergency bit:** who can get into the phone if they can't? Set up the family/recovery/new-emergency-contact feature both platforms have, in front of you, with a real relative named. That is the single step nobody ever regretted.

## Where the "done" bar sits

When you leave the visit, this is what "passwords and backups handled" means, no more no less:

- [ ] One manager, chosen, with a four-word master password (written down once, on paper)
- [ ] Email and bank reset by them, on your watch, with saved passwords turned on
- [ ] Two-factor on for email and bank
- [ ] Phone backup sliders on and showing "backed up today"
- [ ] Restore test of one photo done, together
- [ ] Both teach sheets on the counter, and a next visit booked for whatever's left on the honest list

That last checklist is the actual homework from tonight — and the reason you want the [call log](01-support-call-checklist.md): six months of visits from now, most families are fully covered without anyone ever having felt taught a "lesson."
