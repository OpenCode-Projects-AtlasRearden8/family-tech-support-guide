# When to call the ISP or pay a pro

Part of being the calm helper is knowing which problems were never yours to fix. Handing a problem to the right paid or official channel — kindly, at the right moment, with the right sentences — is a skill of the same dignity as the talk-through fixes. This chapter is the hand-off map.

## The decision table

| The problem | Whose it is | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No internet, and the [two-router reset ritual](04-rescue-order.md) didn't bring it back | **The ISP** | The line into the house is theirs. You've already done the ritual they'll ask for, so the call goes better than average. |
| Internet drops daily, or is slow every evening | **The ISP** | That's a line/outage/equipment pattern they own and document. Say "this is the third drop this month" — patterns get visits. |
| Moving the modem, wiring a room, a router for a big house | **The ISP first, then a pro** | ISPs do installs; a small local tech does it nicer, sometimes both at once. Get both numbers before buying anything. |
| New device setup that's just fiddly (email, printer, TV apps) | **You, on a visit** | This is the bread and butter of this manual — nobody needs to pay for it. |
| Phone/tablet/laptop is physically broken (screen, hinge, water, swollen battery) | **A repair shop** | Stop troubleshooting. Any further "just try it" risks the data inside. Go in *with the backup question first*. |
| Data recovery — deleted files, drowned drive, dead computer | **A pro shop — but check the backups first** | Before paying a stranger, reopen the [backup chapter](05-passwords-and-backups.md) in your head. If the backups worked, it might be a $0 miracle: restore and move on. |
| Virus, pop-ups, or a scam that's already had their password or bank card | **Both: you for cleanup, the bank immediately, a pro if windows still misbehave** | The bank call comes first, always. Then the reset drills in [chapter 6](06-elder-scam-shields.md). If pop-ups survive a full update + full restart, a shop does the deep clean in an afternoon. |
| "Reset to factory" they're scared of doing alone | **You, on a visit** | It deletes everything — it deserves a calm hour and a [backup test](05-passwords-and-backups.md) first, not a rushed Tuesday. |
| Anything talking about legal threats, "license expired," or arrest | **Nobody — it's a scam.** Report and close. | See the [scam chapter](06-elder-scam-shields.md). The words on the screen are the whole scam. |

## Calling the ISP without losing the afternoon

The reset ritual from the rescue order is *literally the first thing their phone script makes you do* — so saying you've done it skips a third of the call:

> "I've already unplugged the modem and router for a full minute and powered on in order, and the internet light is still red. I have the account number and the error lights photographed. What's next?"

Then the four polite levers, in order:

1. **Ask what they see on their end.** They can literally look at your line. *"What does the line show right now?"* — instant, free, and non-salesy answers come out here.
2. **Ask for the outage check first.** *"Is there an outage in my area before we troubleshoot my gear?"*
3. **Then the tech on the line.** Five more minutes is a fair price.
4. **Then the visit.** *"If the line's fine on your end but not mine, let's book a tech to the house."* Tech visits are usually completed for free for real line problems — ask, don't assume.

And the unsentimental retirement of one myth: the ISP's own router usually is fine. People buy a "better router" to fix a line problem about once per decade per household, and it almost never works. Expend the ritual, photograph the lights, and *then* shop only if the ISP confirms the line is clean.

## Choosing (and prepping for) a paid pro

- **Local shop over a storefront in a big-box store**, for computers and phones: the same board-level work usually costs less, sits with a person you can call back, and they'll usually tell you honestly when a machine isn't worth fixing.
- **Before the shop, one question from you:** *"Is the data backed up before anyone opens it?"* If they can't answer crisply, choose another shop. This one line saves more family data than any antivirus ever has.
- **Get the estimate in writing:** what's covered, what isn't, what "diagnostic fee" applies if they decide not to repair. Repairs without a confident written estimate are how a $80 screen becomes a $380 evening.
- **You handle the first call, then fade.** Offer to sit in on the call that books the appointment, then let them walk the device to the counter themselves. The counter person speaks beginner, and it's a teachable too — this is the vocabulary their whole family will use for years.
- **Rates, honestly:** expectations beat promises. Device repairs generally run from roughly $50 (batteries, ports) to a few hundred (screens, boards) — and every shop says the honest sentence *"we'll know after the diagnostic."* Ask for the diagnostic fee up front; it's a fair, small number, and it's refundable against the repair at many shops if you ask.

## The calmest hand-off sentence you have

When a problem isn't yours, it's worth saying straight out:

> "This next part isn't about clever fingers — it's the phone company's line, or it's inside the machine. Here's the call I'd make and the sentence I'd use. Want me to dial with you?"

That sentence keeps the helpfulness *theirs* — they make the call — and keeps the [teaching-without-taking-over rule](02-teach-without-taking-over.md) intact even at the boundary of your skills. Being the calm helper isn't being the person who fixes everything; it's being the person who always knows whose turn it is.
