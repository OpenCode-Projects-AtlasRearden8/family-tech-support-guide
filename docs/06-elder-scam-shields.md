# Elder-scam shields

This is the chapter where you repay every dinner you have ever been fed. Scams aimed at older adults are now industrial, patient, and often run by people reading from scripts in warm-sounding accents — and they are built to defeat exactly the technology chapter this manual is written for: the phone.

The good news is that the defense is short. Nobody argues their way out of a scam with cleverness. People avoid scams with a small set of rehearsed habits and one or two sentences you help them practice until they're boring.

## The five shields (hang these five on the wall)

1. **Your bank never calls to ask for money or codes.** No bank anywhere ever needs a gift card number, a wire "to protect your account," or a code read back over the phone — not the fraud department, not "the security team," nobody. Any money-moving instruction that arrives by call, text, or email is the scam itself, full stop.
2. **Urgency is the tool, not the reason.** "Do it in the next ten minutes or the account is frozen" is not pressure; it is the scam's operating system. Say it plainly to anyone who calls: *"If this is urgent, that's exactly when I pause."* Then use the rehearsed escape hatch until it's a reflex: *"I'm going to hang up and call you back on the number on my card."* Real companies wait. Real family waits. Only the scam needs it in ten minutes.
3. **Verify out-of-band, always — for family too.** Say a grandchild is stuck abroad and a voice like his says "I need you to wire $2,000." Hang up and call the grandchild's real number (from your own contacts, not from the call). With AI now able to fake voices, faces, and video that convince everybody, *the only verification that still means anything is:* hang up and dial the person yourself on your own phone. Add a family code word for real emergencies — a word never texted or typed, just spoken between two people — and agree that any money-moving emergency without the code word is not the family.
4. **The gift card test.** If any part of the story involves buying gift cards (Apple, Target, whatever), that is a scam with 100% certainty. There is no legitimate version, ever — not the IRS, not your bank, not the utility company, not the hospital, and not me. When you hear "gift cards," you can stop listening and start the escape hatch.
5. **Nobody gets access to the computer or the phone.** More in [chapter 3](03-guided-fixes-you-talk-through.md), one sentence here: the remote-access request ("let me log in and fix it for you") is the most expensive sentence a scammer uses. It's a hard no for everyone, including friendly tech support voices.

## The scripts (put real words in their mouth)

The panic isn't that they don't know better — it's that the scam's engineering robs them of their words at the exact moment words are needed. So we drill three lines until they're as automatic as a Hello:

- **The hanging-up line:** *"I don't do banking or computer stuff over the phone. Goodbye."* (No debate, no explaining, tone like brush-your-teeth.)
- **The family check line:** *"Let me call Mom before anything moves. If that's a problem, that tells me what I need to know."* (The scam dies the same second sharing is refused.)
- **The shame-killer line — for *you*, not them:** the first thing they hear from you on a RED call is *"The scammer did the scamming. You just answer phones. Let's lock it down."* People who hide scams from their families lose life savings quietly; the ones who call their grown kid about it first aren't the gullible ones, they're the safe ones. Protect that instinct on purpose.

## Setup you do on a visit (half an hour, lasts years)

- **Turn on spam filtering** on the phone (both platforms have it built in now) and show them how to mark junk texts and calls as junk when one arrives, the first few times together. The filter is not perfect — say that out loud — it is airbags, not seatbelts.
- **Numbers that matter go in contacts with faces:** front-desk real numbers for the bank, the clinic, the pharmacy. If a number isn't in contacts in a house that knows this rule, it records the incoming call as suspicious. Teach the rule plainly: *real people can be called back.*
- **The family code word**, created together on the visit and shared in person, one person at a time — never texted, never saved on the phone, because scammers read the phones they've tricked. The teenagers learn it in person too: family members are exactly the props these scripts love to borrow.
- **The 9 p.m. rule** (come up with your household's own version and name it): any request for money, a code, or access gets *one phone call to a family member before anything moves, at any hour.* Not asking-permission — announcing and being talked to. Nobody in the family may tease anyone about using it.
- **The wallet card** (the [scam teach sheet](teach-sheets/scam-scripts.md)): the hanging-up line, the gift card test, and the code word, printed and taped inside a kitchen cabinet so it isn't decorative.
- **The after-action tour:** the next time a scam text arrives, do not delete it right away. Screenshot it, then walk through the tells together like a puzzle: the misspelled word, the wrong bank, the link whose address doesn't match the words it shows. Then delete it. Ten minutes, twice a year, beats every lecture ever given.

## The AI-era add-on (they'll ask; tell the truth)

Because voices and faces can now be faked convincingly, teach the two-part verify ritual by name: **hang up, then dial the person yourself.** That's it. No analyzing the voice, no asking weird questions on the same call, no "is this really you?" (the fraud software says yes to everything — that's its whole job). It's the hang-up-then-dial move, and it works on the most expensive AI scam there is.

And the counter-shield worth teaching *them*: nobody honest will ever be upset that they verified. Bureaucracies don't mind being called back at known numbers. Families don't mind. The only people who get angry about being hung up on are the people who needed the ten minutes.

## When it already happened (RED, from [chapter 1](01-support-call-checklist.md))

In order, no debate, tonight if needed:

1. **Stop contact.** No more calls from them, no "just one more thing," nothing — politely hang up on the scammer again and again until they stop. (It can take a hard hang-up six times. Each one is the win.)
2. **Call the bank right then** using the number from the card or the branch — not the number the scammer gave. Freeze, cancel, dispute in that order if needed; the fraud line will guide it.
3. **Change the email password immediately** (see [chapter 5](05-passwords-and-backups.md)) if anything at all was typed — codes, "verify your password," anything.
4. **Report it** — the FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov), the local police non-emergency number if money moved, and the phone carrier's spam line. Not for the money back, usually; for the paper trail and the dignity of the record.
5. **Check in again tomorrow.** Scam aftermath gets re-lived in the quiet of the second evening. A call that starts "how did last night go?" is part of the medicine, not a formality.

One sentence worth taping up with the rest: **the shame is the scammer's; the job list is what we do next.** Families that practice that sentence raise the helpers; families that skip it train their parents to hide the next one.
