# Teach without taking over

The pull to just grab the phone and fix it is enormous. You could do it in twenty seconds. Resist it every single time, because of the math nobody does out loud:

**If you do the fix, the call solved one problem. If they do the fix, the call solved every future copy of that problem.**

Taking over also has a cost they feel and never say: it announces *"you are not capable of this."* People quietly retire from the device after that. So this chapter is about the two techniques that let a twenty-second fix become a twenty-second win for them.

## The Hands Rule

**Their hands on the device. Your words, not your fingers.** Not "sometimes," not "when we're in a hurry" — every time, even when it's painful.

- Step across the desk and point *at* the button, never touch it.
- On a phone call: *"Don't tell me the number — write it on the paper and go at your speed. I'm here."*
- If they hand you the phone, hand it back with the same warmth: *"I'm not stealing that from you — we're gonna do it together and this time you drive."*

## The loop: show, do together, do alone, do again

One skill per call or visit. Then this loop — it fires in about three minutes and it works for everything from attaching a photo to resetting a router:

1. **You show it once, narrating easily.** Do it slowly, in *their* view, describing your own hands out loud. *(Ok, my finger goes to the arrow at top of the screen — see that little arrow — and I slide it right.)*
2. **Do it together.** Their device, their hands, your voice alongside: *"Your turn — find the arrow."*
3. **They do it alone** while you stay quiet. Clench your jaw through the mistake if one happens; mistakes are data, not failures. *"Not this time — what does the phone think should happen?"*
4. **They do it a second time** from the start, no coaching. This repetition is the difference between "I think I had it" and "I have it."
5. **Name it.** *"That skill is 'closing an app all the way.' You have it forever."* Naming the skill matters more than it sounds — it gives them a thing to ask for next time by name.

Then **leave the memory behind**: a teach sheet on the counter, or better, a sticky note *in their own handwriting* ("click the green, not the grey"). Your words fade the same evening. Their handwriting is there in the morning.

## Say this, not that

| Instead of | Say |
| --- | --- |
| "It's easy." | "Everyone gets stuck here. Look — the button is grey, that's a clue." |
| "Obviously…" | (Delete the word. There is no version of this you need.) |
| "I told you about this last time." | "You remember the weird part is the grey one, right?" |
| "No, the *other* arrow." | "The list is shy today. Let's have you read me the whole menu first." |
| "You broke it." | "It does something interesting when you do that — let's see what it's telling us." |
| "I'll just do it." | "You're about to learn the adult-password trick. You drive." |

Notice every "say this" line turns the error into a clue. That's not spin — it's literally true, and it's the fastest route to them reading screens instead of fearing them. The phone-call version of these moves — where you can't even point at the screen — is the "be their screen" method in [chapter 3](03-guided-fixes-you-talk-through.md), and one good place to practice the vocabulary is the [glossary](08-glossary.md), which is written to be read out loud.

## The reading-the-screen habit

The single skill with the best return on investment: getting them to *say what the screen says* rather than what they fear.

> "Read me the red letters, word for word, like a news anchor."
> "It says 'Storage almost full — manage.'" 
> "Great. So the phone isn't broken, it's full. Full is the best kind of problem — it means we delete instead of repair."

That swap — from reading feelings to reading text — is most of the difference between a panic call and a puzzle. Teach it on purpose, by asking for the read-aloud every single time.

## Sizing lessons: one thing per visit

The cruel-but-kind limit: **one skill per visit.** Two skills, and by Tuesday they remember nothing. One skill, practiced twice in your presence, remembered a year.

So keep a short list of "next skills" for each person — you'll notice it supports the call log from [chapter 1](01-support-call-checklist.md). On the visit: fix what's needed, teach the one skill, leave the sheet, schedule the next one-skill visit. That pace feels slow in week one and unbeatable by month three.

## When taking over *is* allowed

Two honest exceptions, used rarely and named out loud:

- **Setup chores** nobody needs to learn (typing 40 Wi-Fi characters, walking through a printer's menus looking for the word "castle" in the Wi-Fi password). Say so when you do it: *"This one's a chore, not a skill — I'll do the typing, you do the next part."*
- **RED problems.** If a scam is mid-drain at 7:14 p.m., you act first and teach on Sunday. Life before pedagogy, always.

Everything else is theirs to do — and the clever bit is they'll want to do it, because the other secret of teaching without taking over is that **being shown trust feels exactly like being taught.**
